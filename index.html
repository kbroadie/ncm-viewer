What if AI wrote the way it paints?

AI image tools don’t draw a picture pixel by pixel. They rough in the whole thing and sharpen it. Chatbots still write one word at a time. Researchers at Stanford built a model that writes like a painter, and it’s fast.

Story and illustrations by Kevin BroadieResearch by Hermann Kumbong and colleagues at StanfordNovember 2026

Text has always worked differently. ChatGPT, Gemini, Llama and nearly every other chatbot is a large language model, or LLM. LLMs write by predicting the next token, a word or piece of a word, adding it to the page and doing it again. Each token takes a full trip through the model. A 300-word answer means roughly 400 trips, one after another, and none can start until the last one finishes.

A team at Stanford asked a simple question: what if text could be painted instead of typed? Their answer is a method called Next-Concept Modeling, or NCM. The name riffs on “next-token prediction.” Instead of guessing the next word, NCM guesses the next, sharper version of the whole passage.

Three ways to write a sentence

AI text generators fall into roughly three families, and each maps onto something familiar.

The typewriter is the LLM approach behind today’s chatbots, known in the field as autoregressive generation. The scratch-off card is a newer family called diffusion language models, including Google’s Gemini Diffusion, Inception Labs’ Mercury and the research model LLaDA. They start with every word hidden and reveal a few at a time. The sketch artist is NCM.

What does a rough sentence look like?

A blurry picture is easy to imagine. A blurry sentence is not. So how does NCM rough in text?

It borrows the picture trick. Before writing anything, NCM learns a compact shorthand for text. Think of it as a palette: instead of colors, it holds 4,096 symbols, each standing for a chunk of meaning. A passage is stored as rows of slots, and each slot holds one symbol. The first row has a single slot, the one-block picture of the passage. Each row after it has more slots and corrects what the rows above got wrong, the way the picture went from one block to four to sixteen.

Writing means filling the rows from the top down, one row per pass, with every slot in a row filled at the same moment. Here’s the translation from the race, with its slots.

The translation’s ninth draft, with its “Mexican Mexican,” really is messier than its eighth. The researchers measured this: quality can dip in the middle drafts before the last pass cleans everything up. Anyone who has tidied a room knows the feeling. It always looks worst right before it’s done.

So how much faster is it?

Every pass costs time, so fewer passes should mean faster text. The researchers counted the steps NCM needed against the strongest diffusion model of the same size.

Fewer steps don’t turn into time one-for-one, because each step does a different amount of work. So the researchers also timed both approaches on real hardware.

10×faster on web pages
21×faster on news sentences
31×faster on news summaries

It didn’t give up quality to get there. NCM’s writing scored better than the diffusion model on news sentences, about as well on web pages and just as well on summaries. It even found a use for the time it saved: in the steps a diffusion model spends on one summary, NCM can write 16 and keep the best.

When the researchers tried exactly that, their summaries scored about 7% better.

It’s also weirdly good at Sudoku

One surprising result has nothing to do with words. The team trained NCM on Sudoku: give it a puzzle, and it writes out the solved grid of 81 digits, treating it like a short, very strict sentence.

LLM-style0 of 81 steps

NCM0 of 12 passes
99.5%of easy puzzles solved in 12 passes. NCM beat the LLM-style and diffusion models it was tested against at every difficulty level.

Why might drafting help? Sudoku punishes you for committing to a digit before you’ve considered the whole board. An LLM has to commit to each cell in order and can’t take it back. NCM settles the big picture of the whole grid first and the details last. The paper doesn’t claim that’s the reason, but it fits.

Why your graphics card cares

Here’s something most people don’t know about running an LLM: the slow part usually isn’t the math. It’s memory. To write each token, the computer reads the entire model out of memory, every one of its billions of numbers, and pushes them through the chip. A mid-sized model like Mistral 7B is about 14 gigabytes at standard precision, so a top-end gaming card spends roughly 14 milliseconds per token just moving data while its math units mostly sit idle.

That memory problem is now a supply problem. AI data centers are buying up the world’s fast memory, especially the high-bandwidth memory stacked beside their GPUs. Late in 2025, the head of storage-chip maker Silicon Motion said hard drives, DRAM, high-bandwidth memory and flash storage would all be in severe shortage in 2026, and PC Gamer reported that RAM and SSD prices were already climbing for ordinary buyers. If you’ve priced a graphics card or a RAM upgrade lately, you’ve felt it.

A method that needs fewer trips through memory could help on both ends. In data centers, where companies pack as many users as possible onto each scarce GPU, fewer trips per answer means more answers per chip. At home, where people run models like Llama on a laptop or gaming PC for privacy and offline use, memory speed is exactly what makes local AI feel slow. Drafting in a dozen passes keeps the chip’s idle math units busy instead of waiting on memory.

There are caveats. NCM doesn’t shrink a model, so it still has to fit in your card’s memory. Each pass does more work than a single LLM step. And the paper measures speed, not memory use or energy, so the hardware payoff is a promising implication rather than a proven result.

What it can’t do yet

NCM is a research project, not a product. Its models are small, around 100 million parameters, far smaller than the LLMs behind today’s chatbots, and they’ve been tested on research datasets rather than open-ended conversation. It gives every job the same number of passes, so an easy sentence gets as much effort as a hard one. And its shorthand occasionally trips over names, numbers and accented letters, which matters when the output has to be exact. The summary’s “Stekampkamp” is one example.

Why it matters

People rarely write left to right in one go. We outline, draft and revise. AI learned to paint pictures that way years ago. NCM is a serious attempt to teach it to write that way too, and the early results are fast, accurate and cheaper to train: the researchers report needing roughly a quarter to a fifth as much training text as comparable diffusion models, not counting the training of the shorthand itself.

The big open question is scale. So far the team has tested models up to 725 million parameters, and the results kept improving as the models grew. If that holds at chatbot size, the next AI you talk to might not type at all. It might paint.
