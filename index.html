An AI that writes the whole thing at once, then fixes it

Most AI writes one word at a time. Researchers at Stanford built one that sketches an entire passage first and sharpens it in about a dozen passes. Here’s how it works, and why that matters.

Story and illustrations by Kevin Broadie. Based on research by Hermann Kumbong and colleagues at Stanford. Credits and notes

Ask a chatbot a question and watch the answer appear. It arrives one word at a time, left to right, like someone typing. That isn’t a stylistic choice. Under the hood, the AI really does write one word, reread everything so far, and then write the next one.

A team of researchers at Stanford tried something different. Their method, called Next-Concept Modeling, or NCM for short, doesn’t start at the beginning of the sentence. It starts everywhere at once.

Here’s what that looks like. One of their models is about to write a short children’s story from scratch. Scroll slowly.

Pass 12Done: a complete story about Tim, his kite and a helpful bird, in 12 passes. A typical chatbot would have needed more than a hundred, one for every word or piece of a word.

That’s the whole trick. Instead of writing a sentence the way a typewriter does, NCM writes it the way a painter paints: rough first, then a little sharper, then sharper still, with every part of the canvas getting attention on every pass.

It sounds like a small change. It turns out to make a big difference in speed.

How do you sketch with words?

Sketching a picture is easy to imagine. Sketching a sentence is stranger. What is a “rough” sentence, exactly?

NCM’s answer is a clever kind of compression. Before it writes anything, the system learns to squeeze text into a stack of layers, a lot like the zoom levels on a map.

Web pages32 layers
detail from the layers abovedetail this layer adds
The view from spaceThe top layer is a single code that sums up the whole passage. Think of it as the planet seen from orbit: you can tell land from sea, and not much else.
Zooming inEach layer below adds detail and corrects whatever the layer above got wrong. It’s like tuning a guitar: get it roughly in tune, then make smaller and smaller adjustments.
Street levelThe model trained on web pages uses 32 layers to reach 1,024 slots of detail. A typewriter would need 1,024 steps to fill the same slots. NCM needs 32, one per layer.
Every model is differentThe squeezing is remarkably faithful: unpacking the web-page model’s layers gives back 99.99% of the original words exactly. Tap the buttons to compare other models’ layers.

Layer sizes come from the configuration files the researchers published with their models. To write something new, NCM generates the layers from the top down, filling every spot in a layer at the same moment.

Three models, three jobs

The researchers published three examples, each from a different model with a different job. One writes stories from nothing. One reads a news article and sums it up in a single sentence. One translates German into English.

None of them is a chatbot, so there’s no prompt to type. The story model gets no input at all, and the other two get only the text they’re working on. Along the way, their rough drafts make some wonderfully strange mistakes.

So how much faster is it?

Steps are the expensive part of running an AI, so fewer steps should mean faster answers. The researchers counted how many steps NCM needed compared with the best scratch-off model of the same size.

Fewer steps don’t translate one-for-one into time, because the steps don’t all do the same amount of work. So the researchers also timed both approaches on real hardware.

10×faster on web pages
21×faster on news sentences
31×faster on news summaries

And it didn’t trade quality for that speed. NCM’s writing scored better than the competition on news sentences, about as well on web pages, and just as well on summaries.

The researchers also found a clever use for the time they saved. In the number of steps a scratch-off model spends writing one summary, NCM can write 16 and keep the best one.

When they tried exactly that, their summaries scored about 7% better.

It’s also weirdly good at Sudoku

One of the more surprising results has nothing to do with writing. The team trained NCM to solve Sudoku puzzles, treating the grid like a very short, very strict sentence made of digits.

99.5%of easy puzzles solved, in just 12 passes. NCM beat the typewriter-style and scratch-off models it was tested against at every difficulty level.

One way to think about why: Sudoku punishes you for committing to a square before you’ve looked at the whole board. A typewriter has to commit square by square, in order, with no going back. A sketch artist can rough in the whole grid and then fix the clashes.

What it can’t do yet

Before anyone gets carried away: NCM is a research project, not a product. Its models are small, around 100 million parameters, which is far smaller than the AI behind today’s chatbots. They’ve been tested on research datasets, not on open-ended conversation.

It also gives every job the same number of passes, so an easy sentence gets just as much effort as a hard one. And the compression step occasionally trips over names, numbers and accented letters, which matters when the output has to be exactly right. Stekampkamp, again.

Why this matters

Every word an AI writes costs a burst of computing on a powerful, expensive chip. Multiply that by millions of people asking questions all day, and it adds up to one of the biggest costs, and energy bills, in tech.

Here’s the irony. Those chips are built to do thousands of things at the same time, but typewriter-style writing makes them work through one word at a time, in a line. It’s like owning a 12-lane highway and only ever using one lane.

Typewriter one lane busy, eleven idle
Sketch artist every lane working at once

NCM puts the other lanes to work: fewer steps, each doing lots of work side by side. It may be thriftier to train, too. The researchers report that NCM learned from roughly a quarter to a fifth as much text as comparable scratch-off models, not counting the training of its compression step.

And there’s something satisfying about the idea itself. People rarely write left to right in one go. We outline, draft and revise. AI image generators already work this way, starting blurry and sharpening the picture. NCM is a serious attempt to bring the same habit to words.

The big open question is whether it holds up at the scale of today’s chatbots. So far the team has tested models up to 725 million parameters, and the results kept improving as the models grew. That’s the part to keep an eye on.
