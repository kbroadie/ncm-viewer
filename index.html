An AI that writes the whole thing at once, then fixes it

Most AI writes one word at a time. Researchers at Stanford built one that sketches an entire passage first and sharpens it in about a dozen passes. Here’s how it works, and why that matters.

Story and interactive graphics by Kevin Broadie
Based on research by Hermann Kumbong and colleagues at Stanford. Full credits and notes

Ask a chatbot a question and watch the answer appear. It arrives one word at a time, left to right, like someone typing. That isn’t a stylistic choice. Under the hood, the AI really does write one word, reread everything so far, and then write the next one.

A team of researchers at Stanford tried something different. Their method, called Next-Concept Modeling, or NCM for short, doesn’t start at the beginning of the sentence. It starts everywhere at once.

Here’s what that looks like. Below, one of their models is writing a short children’s story from scratch. Scroll slowly.

That’s the whole trick. Instead of writing a sentence the way a typewriter does, NCM writes it the way a painter paints: rough first, then a little sharper, then sharper still, with every part of the canvas getting attention on every pass.

It sounds like a small change. It turns out to make a big difference in speed.

A typewriter, a scratch-off card and a sketch artist

There are roughly three ways an AI can produce text, and they map neatly onto three familiar things.

The typewriter is how most chatbots work. One word per step, in order, and the steps can’t overlap, because each word depends on the ones before it. A long answer means a long wait.

The scratch-off card is a newer approach called diffusion. It starts with every word hidden and reveals a few at a time, in any order. It works on the whole page at once, which is good, but it usually needs a lot of scratching, often more than a hundred steps.

The sketch artist is NCM. It roughs in the whole passage, then refines all of it in a handful of passes.

How do you sketch with words?

Sketching a picture is easy to imagine. Sketching a sentence is stranger. What is a “rough” sentence, exactly?

NCM’s answer is a clever kind of compression. Before it writes anything, the system learns to squeeze text into a stack of layers, a bit like the zoom levels on a map. The top layer is the view from space: a tiny code that sums up the whole passage. Each layer below zooms in, adding detail and correcting whatever the layer above got wrong. Think of tuning a guitar: get it roughly in tune first, then make smaller and smaller adjustments.

The squeezing is remarkably faithful. When the researchers unpacked the layers for their web-text model, they got back 99.99% of the original words exactly.

To write something new, NCM generates those layers from the top down. Each layer takes one pass, and every spot in a layer gets filled in at the same moment. That’s why the story above appeared everywhere at once.

Anyone who has tidied a room knows the feeling. It always looks worst right before it’s done.

Fewer steps don’t translate one-for-one into time, because the steps don’t all do the same amount of work. So the researchers also timed both approaches on real hardware.

10×faster on web pages
21×faster on news sentences
31×faster on news summaries

And it didn’t trade quality for that speed. NCM’s writing scored better than the competition on news sentences, about as well on web pages, and just as well on summaries.

The researchers also found a clever use for the time they saved. In the number of steps a scratch-off model spends writing one summary, NCM can write 16 and keep the best one. When they tried exactly that, their summaries scored about 7% better.

It’s also weirdly good at Sudoku

One of the more surprising results has nothing to do with writing. The team trained NCM to solve Sudoku puzzles, treating the grid like a very short, very strict sentence made of digits.

99.5%of easy puzzles solved, in just 12 passes. NCM beat the typewriter-style and scratch-off models it was tested against at every difficulty level.

One way to think about why: Sudoku punishes you for committing to a square before you’ve looked at the whole board. A typewriter has to commit square by square, in order, with no going back. A sketch artist gets to rough in the whole grid and then fix the conflicts. The paper doesn’t claim that’s the reason, but it fits.

What it can’t do yet

Before anyone gets carried away: NCM is a research project, not a product. Its models are small, around 100 million parameters, which is far smaller than the AI behind today’s chatbots. They’ve been tested on research datasets, not on open-ended conversation.

It also gives every job the same number of passes, so an easy sentence gets just as much effort as a hard one. And the compression step occasionally trips over names, numbers and accented letters, which matters when the output has to be exactly right. Stekampkamp, again.

Why this matters

Every word an AI writes costs a burst of computing on a powerful and expensive chip. Multiply that by millions of people asking questions all day, and it adds up to one of the biggest costs, and energy bills, in tech.

Here’s the irony. Those chips are built to do thousands of things at the same time, but typewriter-style writing makes them do one word at a time, in a line. It’s like owning a 12-lane highway and only ever using one lane. NCM puts the other lanes to work: fewer steps, each doing a lot of work in parallel.

It may be thriftier to train, too. The researchers report that NCM learned from roughly a quarter to a fifth as much text as comparable scratch-off models, not counting the training of its compression step.

And there’s something satisfying about the idea itself. People rarely write left to right in one go. We outline, draft and revise. AI image generators already work this way, starting blurry and sharpening the picture. NCM is a serious attempt to bring the same habit to words.

The big open question is whether it holds up at the scale of today’s chatbots. So far the team has tested models up to 725 million parameters, and the results kept improving as the models grew. That’s the part to keep an eye on.
