Abstract

Next-Concept Modeling (NCM) [1] generates text from coarse to fine. A multiscale residual tokenizer represents a sequence as a hierarchy of discrete codes, and a transformer predicts that hierarchy one level at a time, filling every position within a level in parallel. The number of sequential forward passes therefore equals the number of levels rather than the number of tokens. This companion makes that process visible. We decode the published samples at every level, chart the level schedules of six released models from their evaluation configurations, and compare the pass counts of autoregressive, diffusion and next-concept generation on a single translation. All figures are interactive, and all sample text and schedules are taken from the public NCM release.

1Introduction

Autoregressive language models produce one token per forward pass, so generation latency grows with output length. Diffusion language models sample positions in parallel, but they revisit the full sequence at every step and typically need many steps to reach good quality [1]. NCM takes a third route: it commits to a short, coarse representation of the entire output and then adds detail over a fixed number of refinement levels. Kumbong et al. report that NCM matches or exceeds strong diffusion baselines while generating 10 to 31 times faster [1].

The intermediate levels of an NCM sample are simple to describe and hard to picture. This page is meant to close that gap. Our contributions are:

  • an interactive decoding of the three published samples, with per-level change highlighting (Section 3);
  • a comparison of level schedules across six released checkpoints, read directly from their configurations (Section 4); and
  • a pass-count comparison that places NCM beside autoregressive and diffusion generation on the same output (Section 5).

2Background

2.1Multiscale residual tokenization

The NCM tokenizer is a VQ-VAE with a multiscale residual quantizer, similar in spirit to next-scale prediction for images [2]. Let z be the encoder's representation of a sequence. The quantizer visits L levels in order, where level ℓ holds nℓ positions and n1 < n2 < … < nL. Schematically, each level quantizes whatever the earlier levels left unexplained:

r1=z, qℓ=Qℓ(Dℓ(rℓ)), rℓ+1=rℓ−Uℓ(qℓ)
(1)

where Dℓ resamples the residual to nℓ positions, Qℓ maps each position to its nearest codebook entry, and Uℓ maps the codes back to full length. The decoder reconstructs the sequence from the sum of all levels. Early levels therefore carry the broad content of the text and later levels carry corrections. The authors report token reconstruction accuracies between 99.79% and 99.99% on the text datasets they evaluate [1].

2.2Level-wise parallel generation

A transformer generator models the code hierarchy level by level. At step ℓ it reads every code from levels 1 through ℓ−1 and predicts all nℓ codes of level ℓ in a single forward pass:

p(q1, …, qL)=∏ℓ=1L p(qℓ | q<ℓ)
(2)

Positions within a level are sampled in parallel, so a complete sample costs L forward passes for a given schedule (n1, …, nL). The released generators use a block-diagonal attention pattern, which the authors found gave the best perplexity and the fastest sampling among the variants they tested [1].

3Refinement in Published Samples

The NCM repository includes an animation of three samples decoded at every level: an unconditional TinyStories [3] story, an XSum [4] summary, and a WMT14 [5] German-to-English translation. We transcribed every level and present them in Figure 1.

Three patterns recur across the samples. First, the earliest levels contain mostly high-frequency function words and punctuation, consistent with level 1 holding very few positions. Second, content words that anchor the meaning, such as names (Tim, Pistorius) and topic nouns (kite, prison, security), appear several levels before the text is grammatical. Third, word order and spelling resolve last: the XSum sample reads Stekampkamp at levels 7 and 8 and Steenkamp only at level 9.

3.1Refinement is not monotonic

Decoded quality does not improve at every level. In the WMT14 sample, levels 9 and 10 repeat words (Mexican Mexican, battle battle) that level 8 did not contain, and level 11 resolves them. Kumbong et al. observe the same effect quantitatively, with decoded BLEU dropping at some intermediate levels before recovering [1].

The schedules differ in shape. TinyStories and LM1B share a 12-level schedule that adds between 7 and 20 positions per level. The Sudoku schedule grows roughly geometrically, from 1 to 89 positions, over a codebook of only 16 entries. OpenWebText uses 32 levels whose lengths are the perfect squares 1, 4, 9, …, 1,024, so each level adds an odd number of positions that grows linearly. The authors find that schedules with the same number of levels and the same endpoints can still train to different quality, so how length is distributed across levels matters [1].

6Limitations

This page is a visualization, not a replication. The per-level text is transcribed from an animation in the NCM release rather than generated by us, so transcription errors are possible. Change highlighting uses a word-level longest-common-subsequence diff, which approximates, but does not reproduce, the highlighting in the original animation. The autoregressive and diffusion lanes in Figure 3 are illustrative simulations. Producing new samples, including per-level outputs for the Sudoku models, requires running the released checkpoints on a GPU, which we leave to future work.

7Conclusion

NCM replaces a token-by-token loop with a short, fixed sequence of parallel refinements. Watching a sample move through its levels shows both what that buys and what it costs: the outline arrives within the first few passes, and the details, including some temporary mistakes, are settled in the last ones.
