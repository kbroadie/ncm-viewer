Coarse first.
Then fine.

Next-Concept Modeling (NCM) is a method for generating text developed by researchers at Stanford. Rather than writing one word after another, it produces a rough draft of the entire passage and then refines that draft in a small, fixed number of passes. This page explains how the method works and lets you watch it happen.

1

Writing in drafts

Most language models, including the chatbots in common use, generate text one piece at a time. Each new word requires a full pass through the model, so a longer answer takes proportionally longer to produce.

NCM works differently. It first settles on a coarse outline of the whole passage, containing only a handful of words. It then makes a series of refinement passes. Each pass adds detail everywhere in the text at once, and the number of passes is fixed in advance, regardless of how long the text is.

TechnicalHow the passes work

Language models operate on tokens, which are words or fragments of words. The standard design, the autoregressive model, predicts each token from all the tokens before it, so producing n tokens takes n sequential forward passes. Diffusion language models instead begin from a fully masked sequence and unmask it over many steps. Each step predicts every position in parallel, but each step also reprocesses the full sequence.

NCM divides the problem between two models. A tokenizer compresses text into a stack of levels, short and coarse at the top, long and detailed at the bottom. A generator then predicts that stack one level at a time, producing every position within a level simultaneously. A sample therefore costs one forward pass per level: 11 passes for the translation in Figure 1, against one pass per token for an autoregressive model.

ExpertFactorization and attention

The generator factorizes the joint distribution over code levels q1, …, qL as

p(q1, …, qL)=∏ℓ=1L p(qℓ | q<ℓ)
(1)

and samples all nℓ codes of level ℓ in one forward pass, so codes within a level are conditionally independent given the earlier levels. The released generators are transformers of roughly 100M parameters with a block-diagonal attention pattern, which the authors found gave the best perplexity and the fastest sampling among the patterns they compared.

Decoded quality is not monotonic in depth. In the WMT14 sample, passes 9 and 10 introduce repetitions (Mexican Mexican, battle battle) that pass 11 removes, and the paper reports a corresponding dip in decoded BLEU at intermediate levels.

2

Turning text into levels

For the generator to work from coarse to fine, the text must first be represented that way. NCM's tokenizer converts a passage into a series of levels. The first level is a very short code. Each following level is longer and records what the levels above it got wrong, so every added level makes the reconstruction more exact.

A progressive image download is a useful comparison: it shows a blurred version of a photograph first and sharpens it as more data arrives. NCM applies the same principle to text.

Each model fixes the length of every level in advance. Figure 2 shows these schedules for six of the released models.

TechnicalCodebooks and residuals

The tokenizer is a vector-quantized autoencoder (VQ-VAE). Each position in a level is stored as an index into a codebook, a learned list of vectors; the text models use codebooks of 4,096 or 8,192 entries. Quantization is residual: each level encodes the difference between the input representation and the reconstruction from all previous levels.

The schedules differ by model. TinyStories and LM1B use 12 levels that grow to 128 positions. OpenWebText uses 32 levels whose lengths are the perfect squares 1, 4, 9, …, 1,024. The Sudoku model uses 12 levels and a codebook of only 16 entries.

ExpertQuantizer, training and schedule design

Schematically, with encoder output z:

r1=z, qℓ=Qℓ(Dℓ(rℓ)), rℓ+1=rℓ−Uℓ(qℓ)
(2)

where Dℓ resamples to nℓ positions, Qℓ is nearest-neighbor quantization against level ℓ's codebook, and Uℓ maps back to full length. The construction resembles the multiscale residual quantizer of VAR (Tian et al., 2024). This equation is a summary for orientation, not the paper's exact formulation.

Training applies nested dropout to the fine levels, which pushes global information into the coarse ones. Reported token reconstruction accuracy ranges from 99.79% on XSum to 99.99% on OpenWebText. The tokenizer is trained separately and then frozen, so it cannot adapt to the generator's objective.

On schedule design, the authors find that additional levels improve generative perplexity with diminishing returns while adding sequential depth, and that schedules with equal depth and equal endpoints can still train to different quality. The distribution of length across levels therefore matters, not only the number of levels.

TechnicalPasses versus time

Pass counts and wall-clock time are related but not identical. An autoregressive pass over a key-value cache processes only the newest token. A diffusion pass processes the entire sequence. An NCM pass processes the current level while attending to the levels already generated. The paper therefore reports both measures. ExpertBaselines and caveats

Comparisons are at matched parameter scale against the strongest diffusion baseline for each task, DUO on LM1B and OpenWebText. NCM achieves better generative perplexity on LM1B and is competitive on OpenWebText. On XSum it matches the strongest diffusion model's ROUGE-L, and with 8 function evaluations it exceeds the unguided AURORA-LM baseline at 128. On WMT14 it uses 11 evaluations without guidance but trails the strongest diffusion baseline, E2D2.

The authors note two limits on these comparisons. Some baseline results are inherited from prior work, and some timing baselines were randomly initialized because released checkpoints were unavailable, which weakens absolute wall-clock claims.

4

Current limits

NCM is a research method, not a product. The published models are small, around 100 million parameters, and are trained on standard research datasets. Every input receives the same number of passes, whether the text is easy or difficult to produce. Some intermediate drafts also become temporarily worse before the final pass, as the translation in Figure 1 shows at passes 9 and 10.

TechnicalWhere errors come from

Because the tokenizer is fixed before the generator is trained, its mistakes become the generator's mistakes. The paper identifies named entities, numerals, diacritics, markup and word boundaries as weak points, which matter most in tasks that require exact output. Errors at coarse levels can also propagate to finer ones.

ExpertScale and adaptivity

Scaling has been tested up to 725M parameters on LM1B, where generative perplexity improves with size while sample entropy remains high. Hierarchy depth and level lengths are fixed per model rather than adapted per input, so compute does not scale with difficulty. The deterministic quantizer also limits how far the method's ELBO interpretation applies.
